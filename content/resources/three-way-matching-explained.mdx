Three-way matching is a simple question with useful consequences: does the invoice describe what was ordered, what was received, and what the supplier is asking to be paid for?

The match is not a substitute for judgement. It gives a controller a consistent starting point and makes the disagreement visible before a bill is approved or posted.

## The three documents

The purchase order describes the commitment: supplier, items, quantities, and agreed prices. The receipt or delivery record describes what arrived. The invoice describes what the supplier wants paid, including tax, totals, and payment details.

When those records agree, the bill has a clear path forward. When they do not, the difference is the work: a short delivery, a changed price, a duplicate invoice, or a document that belongs to another order.

## What the check compares

The useful comparison is not just a single total. A review can look at supplier identity, line items, quantities, prices, tax, currency, dates, and the tolerance your workflow allows. A small rounding difference may be acceptable; an unexplained quantity change may not be.

Two-way matching compares an invoice with a purchase order. Three-way matching adds evidence that the goods or services were received. The extra document matters most when the cost of paying for the wrong thing is higher than the time needed to check it.

## When the documents disagree

The right outcome is a clear exception, not a forced match. Show the fields that differ, keep the source documents close, and send the bill to the person who can resolve that particular question. A controller may accept a documented variance, ask for a corrected invoice, or wait for the delivery record.

This is also where other controls matter. A matched invoice can still have changed bank details or look like a duplicate submission. Matching is one control in the review chain, not a guarantee that a bill is safe.

## A useful review sequence

1. Confirm that the supplier and purchase order are the same relationship.
2. Compare the relevant lines, quantities, prices, tax, and currency.
3. Check that the receipt covers what the invoice asks to bill.
4. Resolve or approve the variance with a recorded reason.
